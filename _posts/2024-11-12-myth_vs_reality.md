title: "Mythbusters: 32-Hour Workweek Edition"
---

When we talk to people about the 32 hour work week campaign, we hear a lot of positive feedback:

- "Wow, this would give me so much more time with my kids."
- "This would make it so much easier to schedule doctor’s appointments."
- "It would be so great to get an extra day to keep up with chores. Maybe my house could actually stay clean!"

But many people, understandably, have questions. So we wanted to bust some myths about this movement. 

1. **I’d like more time off, but I don’t want to take a pay cut or lose my pension.**
We feel the same way! We are fighting for a 32 hour workweek _without_ a reduction in pay or benefits - and we don’t want our negotiations team to settle for less. The point of this campaign is to get the state to recognize 32 hours as the standard, full-time work week, and compensate workers accordingly. We will not support a reduction of hours that’s tied to a reduction in compensation. 

2. **Won’t this feed into negative stereotypes about government employees being lazy?**
The 32 hour workweek is [popular with the public](https://www.dataforprogress.org/datasets/polling-on-32-hour-work-week) - people generally think that employees across sectors (including the government) deserve it. The call for a 32 hour work week is echoing across industries, including manufacturing, home care, police departments, nonprofit workers, and more. So while we don’t really think that many people see government workers as lazy, if someone DOES think thatIS going to call us lazy, they’re going to have to call manufacturing employees, home care workers, police, and many nonprofit workers lazy too. 

3. **There’s no way my coworkers and I can get our work done in 32 hours.** 
That may be true! The 32-hour workweek will look different for different agencies, job duties, and even seasons. While some people might be working a four-day week M-Th, others might have their 32-hours spread across the week to accommodate for being on-call or ensuring that a specific area is always staffed 24/7. The important aspect is that 32-hours is considered full-time and people would be compensated with overtime for any hours they work beyond the 32 hours - and that management is staffing your agency so that their employees can accomplish the work they’ve agreed on in the time given. 

4. **Isn’t this basically giving us a raise to do less work?**
Nope! We’ve actually put in more work over the past 30 years than ever before, and been paid less by comparison - U.S. employees have increased productivity by 400% since 1940. The average household in 1975 worked 2,800 hours annually. Now the average is 3,400 hours. That is a 600 hour increase.
This movement is about recognizing the value of the work that employees are _already doing_ and rewarding them with the time that they deserve for that hard work. 

5. **Seems like something that will only work for salaried office/telework employees.**
“Knowledge work” (e.g. office work) may be one of the easiest places to start moving to the 32 hour work week - but shift work or on-call jobs already manage scheduling around changing demands. Moving from 40-hours for full time work to 32-hours is attainableour goal is to extend this benefit to all employees in our union (and eventually, all workers across Minnesota)! For some types of work, some of the easiest steps are to eliminate unnecessary meetings, distractions and interruptions, and recognizing how technology we’re already using is allowing us to complete work more quickly. But we can make this happen for other types of work as well - it’s just a matter of designing it the right way. We’re already seeing the 32-hour workweek work for industries that might seem challenging, like 24/7 emergency services - Police Departments in [Ohio](https://www.cleveland.com/news/2019/12/newburgh-heights-approves-32-hour-work-week-with-no-pay-cut-for-village-employees.html) and [Colorado](https://www.cbsnews.com/colorado/news/golden-police-department-4-day-work-week-productivity-up-better-community-connection/) have made the switch. As have healthcare and senior care employers in [New Jersey](https://www.npr.org/2024/06/26/nx-s1-5013956/4-four-day-week-work-life-balance-nurse-managers) and [Wisconsin](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hbHzrdHSZGc).
